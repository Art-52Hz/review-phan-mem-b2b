Choose hosting around the application you need to run and the person who will maintain it. A larger server allocation does not, by itself, prove faster pages or lower operating cost.

**Updated October 8, 2026:** This is a decision checklist based on documentation, not a comparative hosting benchmark. Earlier unsupported speed percentages, compliance assurances and universal refund promises have been removed.

## Start with the requirement that changes the decision

| Requirement | Ask about shared hosting | Ask about a VPS |
|---|---|---|
| Application runtime | Does the plan support your language, version and background tasks? | Can you install the required runtime, and who maintains it? |
| Resource limits | What CPU, memory, process, database and storage limits apply? | Which allocations are shared, reserved or burstable under this specific plan? |
| Administration | Which server tasks are included and which application tasks remain yours? | Who patches the OS, controls access and responds to incidents? |
| Recovery | How are backups retained and restored? | Who tests restoration and keeps a usable recovery copy? |
| Budget | What are renewal, upgrade and add-on costs? | What are server, license, backup, monitoring and operator costs? |

Shared hosting can remain a candidate when it supports the workload and its limits are acceptable. A VPS becomes worth evaluating when a required service, configuration or resource cannot be accommodated by the existing plan. Neither label is enough to settle the decision.

Cloudflare's [cloud infrastructure explanation](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/cloud/what-is-the-cloud/) describes virtualization and multitenancy. Treat the precise allocation and isolation of a hosting product as something to verify in its order documentation; do not assume that every VPS provides dedicated physical CPU cores.

## Diagnose before migrating

Write down a concrete problem: a failed background job, an unsupported runtime, a documented limit or a slow client journey. Record the affected URL or task, time window and reproducible steps. Ask whether caching, image size, application queries or the current plan's configuration contributes to it.

Use the same workload and acceptance conditions when evaluating an alternative. Keep unmeasured response time and capacity unknown. A migration is not evidence of improvement until the relevant journey works under the agreed conditions.

## Put an operating owner in the quote

For each candidate, name the person responsible for access, updates, monitoring, backups, restoration and incident communication. Ask a managed provider for the specific tasks included rather than interpreting “managed” as unlimited application support.

For a client website, use the [managed WordPress responsibility checklist](/posts/managed-wordpress-vps-hosting-2026/) and the [WordPress handoff worksheet](/posts/wordpress-client-site-transfer-checklist/). Include the time required to maintain the site in the decision, even when the server invoice is small.

## Compare the complete order

Request the initial term, renewal amount, taxes, licenses, control panel, backup storage, transfer charges and migration support in writing. Confirm cancellation and refund eligibility for the exact product and payment method before paying.

## Set migration acceptance criteria

Before changing DNS, agree on:

- The receiving owner and approved access list.
- A recoverable backup and the person who can restore it.
- The pages, forms, email and integrations to check.
- A change window, rollback condition and communication contact.
- The final recurring cost and responsibility for future renewal.

Record each result as passed, failed or not tested. Keep the previous service available according to the agreed rollback plan until acceptance is complete. This checklist does not claim a migration has been performed.
